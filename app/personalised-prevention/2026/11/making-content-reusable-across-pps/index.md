This design history describes how we used the blood pressure reporting journey to explore how content could be reused across Personalised Prevention Services (PPS). It builds on our earlier work to create a [shared view of PPS capabilities](https://design-history.prevention-services.nhs.uk/personalised-prevention/2026/06/shared-view-capabilities/) and the [blood pressure journey and reusability spike](https://design-history.prevention-services.nhs.uk/personalised-prevention/2026/09/blood-pressure-journey-spike/). We wanted to understand what content reuse would mean in practice, beyond reusing existing journeys or individual pieces of content.

## Context

PPS services are at different stages of maturity and have varying levels of content design support. Teams have already invested significant time and expertise in creating content that meets user needs, supports clinical safety and follows NHS standards. As the portfolio grows, we want to maximise the value of that work and avoid duplicating effort or creating inconsistent experiences. We initially expected that comparing content across services would reveal opportunities for reuse.

We reviewed existing service journeys and research, spoke to delivery teams and mapped where similar content problems appeared. Although this revealed recurring themes, it did not give us enough detail to understand what reuse would mean in practice: what should remain consistent, what could be adapted or how another team could apply the learning.

We needed to work through a real journey to understand what could be reused, what needed to vary and how teams could apply the learning in practice.

## Using blood pressure as a test case

The [blood pressure spike](https://design-history.prevention-services.nhs.uk/personalised-prevention/2026/09/blood-pressure-journey-spike/) gave us an opportunity to test this thinking through a real journey. Blood pressure reporting appears across several PPS services and was already being explored as a technically reusable capability, partly because other teams were interested in using it as a standalone service. It also brought together wider content challenges, including completing activities offline, leaving and returning to a journey, validating information, handling safety concerns and explaining results.

Mapping the end-to-end journey helped us distinguish between problems specific to blood pressure and problems likely to appear elsewhere in PPS. For example, the challenge of leaving a digital service to complete an activity offline is not unique to blood pressure. Looking across these problems showed that reuse depended not only on the subject matter, but on the role different content played in the journey.

## What we found

Working through the journey showed us that content reuse did not mean making all content consistent. We needed to distinguish between content that should remain stable, content that could adapt and content that needed to remain local. We separated content into three broad categories:

- a clinically assured core that should remain consistent across services, unless it is reviewed and updated through the appropriate assurance process

- adaptable behavioural content that can respond to different users, services and contexts

- service-specific or local content that reflects delivery arrangements, available services or channel requirements

For example, the clinically assured explanation of what a blood pressure result means could form part of the stable clinical core. The way a service supports someone to obtain a reading could be adapted to its users and context. Information about where someone can get a reading would usually need to be provided locally.

This helped us identify what teams could reuse consistently, what they could adapt and what they would need to provide for their own service or context.

We also grouped recurring content challenges into four initial pattern categories:

1. Input: helping users understand, obtain and enter the information a service needs

2. Stop and return: supporting users to leave a digital journey, complete an activity elsewhere and return later

3. System handling: validation, error messaging and how the service handles their information

4. Meaning and outcomes: helping users understand what their information or result means and what they can do next

Each pattern can contain a combination of stable, adaptable and local content, depending on the problem it addresses.

Rather than prescribing content, the patterns are intended to provide a reusable structure that teams can apply to similar problems. A completed pattern would describe the problem, when it applies, what should remain stable, what can be adapted or provided locally, and relevant examples and evidence from services.

One recurring challenge was supporting users to leave a service to obtain a blood pressure reading and then return to complete their journey.

At first, this appeared to be a reminder-content problem. Journey mapping showed that users needed more than a reminder. They needed to understand why the reading mattered, know how to obtain one, feel confident leaving the service and reconnect with the task when they returned.

This shifted the focus from designing a reminder message to considering the whole stop-and-return user experience.

The reusable element was not the wording of an individual message. It was the structure of the experience around it: preparing the user to leave, supporting them to complete the activity and helping them reconnect with the task when they returned. The same structure could potentially be applied to other journeys where users need to leave a digital service, complete an activity elsewhere and return later.

## What we learned

Three main findings emerged from the work:

1. Start with a shared user problem, not an existing piece of content. Working through blood pressure reporting helped us see where services faced the same underlying challenge, even when their journeys and wording differed.

2. Reuse requires clear boundaries. Teams need to know which content should remain consistent, which elements they can adapt and which information they must provide for their own service or local context.

3. Patterns need enough context to be used beyond their original service. A pattern should explain the problem, when it applies, the evidence behind it and where teams have flexibility. We still need to test whether another team can understand and apply the patterns without support from their original authors.

The work also raised questions about ownership and governance. Teams need enough flexibility to respond to their users and service context, while changes to clinically assured content need appropriate oversight. Our emerging direction is to make ownership and change controls proportionate to the type of content: stable clinical content would require stronger assurance, while delivery teams could retain greater ownership of adaptable behavioural and service-specific or local content.

This model needs further definition and testing before it can be adopted across PPS.

## What happens next

This work gave us a model and an initial set of patterns to test, rather than finished standards.

We now need to find out whether another PPS team can apply a pattern without support from its original authors, whether the boundaries between stable, adaptable and service-specific or local content hold up in practice, and how teams can contribute evidence and improvements.

This would help us establish a proportionate approach to ownership, assurance and maintenance before the patterns are used more widely across PPS.
