As part of our work on a [digital service for lung cancer screening](https://www.digital-prevention-services.nhs.uk/screening/lung/), we ran two co-design workshops with people who smoke or have smoked shisha. Our earlier design history, [Why and how we used co-design to understand shisha smoking history](/lung-health-check/2026/10/why-and-how-we-used-codesign/), explains how we designed the workshops.

This post looks at another important part of the research: working with community organisations and community leaders to plan and run the workshops.

We worked with [RM Partners NHS Cancer Alliance](https://rmpartners.nhs.uk/), who connected us with Mehr Health CIC and the Iranian Association in Hammersmith, and Amanah Health and Wellbeing CIC in Croydon. These organisations already had strong links with communities we wanted to hear from. They understood how best to involve people in the research.

## Community organisations helped us reach people

In our earlier research, we had found it difficult to recruit people aged 50 and over who had experience of smoking shisha.

For the workshops, community organisations helped us find and invite people from their existing community groups. This meant that people heard about the research from organisations and community leaders they already knew.

The organisations also:

- provided familiar community venues
- advised us on the best days and times to meet
- reviewed our participant information
- introduced the NHS team to participants
- helped us run activities
- supported conversations around the tables
- translated where needed

## Local knowledge changed how we planned the workshops

Working with community leaders helped us spot practical issues we could easily have missed.

For the Hammersmith workshop, Mehr Health CIC offered to translate our written materials into Farsi. This included recruitment information, workshop materials and the summary we shared with participants after the workshop.

This meant people could read information and take part using the language they were most comfortable with. We did not have to assume that everyone could or wanted to work in English.

For the Croydon workshop, Amanah Health and Wellbeing CIC told us that our proposed 1pm to 3pm workshop would clash with Dhuhr, an early afternoon Islamic prayer. We changed the workshop time as a result.

Neither issue would necessarily have been clear to our research team. Working with community leaders early helped us avoid creating barriers for people who wanted to take part.

## Community leaders helped us run the workshops

We did not want community organisations to find participants and then step away from the research.

Community leaders stayed involved on the day. They welcomed participants, introduced the NHS team, explained activities, translated where needed and joined discussions at the tables.

This was particularly useful because people were talking about personal subjects, including smoking, family, culture, faith and health.

Having familiar people in the room helped people share their experiences. After one workshop, the community organisation told us that people had felt comfortable talking about their cultural perspectives and personal experiences. They also told us that our approach had helped people talk openly about shisha.

## Local knowledge helped us make the research more appropriate

Our community partners also helped us decide how to structure the research.

When we planned our Croydon workshop, our community partner told us that some people would be more comfortable talking about their experiences in separate sessions for men and women.

We followed this advice instead of asking everyone to take part in one mixed session.

Our first Croydon workshop was therefore mostly with men. We then planned an additional informal session with women from the community, giving them a separate space to share their experiences.

This reminded us that inclusive research does not always mean bringing everyone into the same room. Sometimes we need to change how we run research so that people can take part in a way that works for them.

Without advice from our community partner, we may not have known this before we started recruiting.

## What we would do again

Working with community organisations helped us run research that was more accessible and appropriate for the people taking part.

For future community-based research, we would:

- involve community organisations before recruitment starts
- use local knowledge when planning the research, not just when finding participants
- ask community partners to review our language, timings, venues and participant information
- plan translation with community partners rather than assuming everyone can take part in English
- invite community leaders to help run sessions where this helps participants
- listen to advice about how different groups may feel most comfortable taking part
- share what we learned with the communities afterwards

We initially needed community organisations to help us reach participants, but their contribution went much further. Their local knowledge helped shape the research from planning through to running the workshops. They helped us reach people we had previously struggled to hear from. More importantly, they helped us plan and run the research in ways that worked better for the people taking part.
