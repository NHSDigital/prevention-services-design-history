From April to October 2026 we ran a pilot for [a new digital service for the lung cancer screening programme](https://www.digital-prevention-services.nhs.uk/screening/lung/). We wanted to test the feasibility of an online assessment questionnaire and verify that we could collect accurate data from participants. We invited eligible participants to test our new service before they completed their [lung health check](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/lung-cancer-screening/) by phone. 

We’re currently analysing the data we gathered during pilot and will be sharing our insights with our pilot partners and the lung cancer screening programme, as well as the wider digital services prevention portfolio. But in the meantime we’ve been reflecting on what went well, and not-so-well.  

Our most important lessons were to: 

- be clear about what a pilot can and cannot tell us 
- involve data specialists earlier 
- plan for the processes and dependencies that sit outside our immediate team 


## Manage expectations from the start

When we were planning our pilot we were clear, within our team, on what the purpose was. We wanted to test if participants were able to complete our online assessment and understand how comparable their responses were to the existing phone service. This seemed simple enough. But we could have been clearer, especially with our pilot partners and the programme, on what the pilot was not going to tell us.  

From the start of our pilot: 

- we decided not to calculate a participant’s risk score based on their digital responses 
- we did not want to assume that a participant’s phone responses were ‘correct’ and that participant responses from our digital service should match them exactly 
- we did not want the insights of the pilot to be seen as the deciding factor for whether a digital service would be an appropriate alternative to the phone service 
- our intention was always to test, learn from the pilot and iterate. This is a different approach to how the programme usually procures software. And we could have communicated this more often.  

## Be clear about the difference between research and clinical validation

When we launched our pilot we planned to include 3 ‘pauses’. During these ‘pauses’ we would stop inviting new participants to the service and think about what design iterations we would like to test.  

However, shortly after launch we decided not to pause the pilot at any point. The idea was that we would test the same designs for the duration of pilot, giving us consistent data to compare to the phone service. The benefit was that this would give us evidence on agreement or differences between the 2 services, usability, and where further research might be needed.  

An unintended consequence of framing the pilot in this way was that we started to give the impression of a rigorous scientific and clinical comparison of the digital and phone assessments. But this was never our intention when we launched pilot.  

When we shared interim findings mid-way through the pilot we were transparent about what we were testing and what we had learnt so far. And given our early success in testing with over 300 participants it was easy for stakeholders and subject matter experts to get excited about the potential of a digital service. We were sometimes asked questions about whether we would publish our research in academic journals. We could have communicated the nuance between our quantitative user research and clinical validation more clearly.  

## Consider data analysis when designing your pilot 

One of the hardest lessons we learnt was that our data analyst should have been involved when we designed our questionnaire. We moved at pace to design and deliver our pilot for a digital risk assessment. Our attention was focused on designing and delivering a risk assessment that met user needs and could test our assumptions without disrupting the existing service.  

Once our pilot launched we onboarded our data analyst to help us with the data we were collecting. But we soon realised that they should have joined the team much earlier. Whilst we were confident our designs worked for users because we had tested and iterated them multiple times. We had not considered the practicalities of how the data our service collected would be compared to the phone data we received from our pilot partners.  

Had our data analyst been on the team when we were planning the pilot they could’ve flagged the differences in how we collected data compared to our pilot partners. We could have worked together on comparing the 2 data collection methods. Iterating our approach to collecting this information.    

## Information governance is a team sport 

We thought we were well prepared with our information governance (IG), having started drafting our data protection impact assessment (DPIA) when kicking off the project. But what we hadn’t considered was that our pilot partners and their third-party provider would have their own IG and DPIA processes. Our pilot was delayed by nearly a month because we hadn’t included our partners’ review processes, and changes arising from them, within our timeline. A lesson that seems obvious in hindsight, and one we won’t be making again.  

## Small but notable lessons 
### Provide a deadline for incentives
We provided an incentive to users who chose to test our digital service, but we did not include a deadline or a timeframe by which they must complete it. Thankfully this hasn’t caused any issues, but it should’ve been clearly stated.   

### Do not provide your team’s contact details if you’re not set up to receive sensitive information 

We included our team’s email address on our ‘contact us’ page and received an email with personally identifiable information in it. This was not information our team should have access to and we therefore needed to raise it as an incident and follow the necessary protocols. We have since updated the email to contact information for a pilot partners as they are the most suitable contact point for any participants.  

### Expect the unexpected delays 

We ended up running our pilot for 7 months rather than the 3 we planned for.  Our pilot partners started calling participants from GP practices that we agreements in place for to test the digital service. Meaning that these participants would have completed the phone service before testing our digital questionnaire, influencing and therefore invalidating their digital responses.  

We’re grateful that our partners worked hard to correct this mistake, recruiting additional GP practices to take part in our pilot. We had built strong relationships with our partners, holding weekly catch ups, working openly and collaboratively with them. Something we will be doing again in any future pilot. It helped ensure the pilot was still a success despite the delays.    

## Our successes 

There are, of course, many successful outcomes from our pilot that we are celebrating: 

- 622 participants completed both the digital and phone service 
- built strong relationships with RM Partners West London cancer alliance and InHealth 
- built trust within the lung cancer screening programme that we can deliver a digital service 
- successful collaboration with clinical colleagues to balance documentation with the lower levels of risk of a test and learn project 

In the spirit of working in the open we hope that sharing our lessons learnt will be helpful to other teams working in similar spaces.
