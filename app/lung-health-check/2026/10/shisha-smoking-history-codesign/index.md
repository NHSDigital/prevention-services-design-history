As part of our work on a digital service for [lung cancer screening,](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/lung-cancer-screening/) we need to find a way for people who smoke or have smoked shisha to describe their smoking history accurately. Our earlier research had already shown that [shisha smoking does not always fit questions designed around cigarette use,](app/lung-health-check/2026/07/understanding-shisha/index.md) so we worked directly with people with lived experience of shisha to explore what a better approach could look like. Through two co-design workshops, we learned more about how people remember their shisha use, what makes their smoking histories difficult to describe, and what this means for the questions we design.

## How we worked with communities

We ran two in-person co-design workshops with people aged 50 and over who currently or previously smoked shisha. Our previous design history, [Why and how we used co-design to understand shisha smoking history,](app/lung-health-check/2026/10/why-and-how-we-used-codesign/index.md) explains how we worked with community organisations in Hammersmith and Croydon, and how we designed and ran the workshops.

## What we learned

### Shisha histories are rarely simple or continuous

People told us about periods when they smoked more, smoked less, stopped altogether and started again.

Someone might have smoked regularly when they were younger, stopped for several years, and later returned to occasional shisha at family or social gatherings.

This reinforced a problem with simply asking someone how many years they smoked shisha. A period of 10 years between starting and stopping does not necessarily represent 10 years of continuous use.

Our questions therefore need to allow for changing smoking patterns, including long breaks, restarts and periods of different levels of use.

### People remember smoking through life events

The timeline activity showed us that people did not necessarily remember their shisha use through dates.

Instead, they often anchored their memories around significant events, including:

- moving country
- marriage
- pregnancy or having children
- changing friendship groups
- taking up sport
- COVID-19
- developing health problems

These events often marked significant changes in how much someone smoked or whether they smoked at all.

This suggests that supporting people to think about different periods of their lives may help them remember complicated smoking histories more accurately.

### Many people started young

Across both workshops, participants often described first encountering shisha as teenagers or young adults.

Friends and family were important influences. People talked about curiosity, wanting to fit in, seeing family members smoking and shisha being part of social or cultural life.

By the time people reach lung cancer screening age, their current shisha use may therefore look very different from how much they smoked in the past.

This highlights a distinction between primary prevention (preventing people from starting) and early diagnosis. Prevention campaigns may need to reach people when they are much younger, while screening needs to help people accurately reconstruct behaviour that may have happened decades earlier.

### “How much?” can be a difficult question

There was enormous variation in what it meant to smoke shisha.

Some people had tried it once or twice. Others described weekly or more frequent use. A person's pattern could also change significantly throughout their life.

When participants designed their own questions, they generally broke the problem into smaller pieces: when somebody started, how often they smoked, how long they smoked for, whether they had breaks, and whether they still smoked.

This suggests that asking several simpler questions may produce better information than asking people to calculate a single lifetime amount themselves.

### Context matters, even when we do not need to collect it

When asked to design questions, participants frequently wanted to understand why people smoked, who they smoked with and where they smoked.

They mentioned friends, family, cafés, celebrations, relaxation, stress and cultural traditions.

We may not need to collect all of this information for the risk calculation. But understanding that context helps us design questions that reflect how people themselves think about shisha.

### Language and recognition matter

Different communities used different words for shisha, like hookah, nargileh and ringela. Participants suggested using alternative names and pictures to make it clear what we are asking about.

Participants also highlighted the importance of explaining why the NHS wants this information.

This matters because [previous research has shown that people may not always think of shisha in the same way as cigarette smoking.](app/lung-health-check/2026/07/understanding-shisha/index.md) If people do not recognise their behaviour as relevant to a question, they may not report it.

Our questions therefore need to use clear, recognisable terminology, with alternative names where they help people understand what we mean. We also need to explain why we are asking, so people understand why their historical shisha use is relevant and how the information will be used.

## What happens next

We will use these findings to improve how we collect shisha smoking history within the digital assessment.

We will then share our response back with participants and community partners, showing what we changed as a result of what they told us and giving them another opportunity to provide feedback.

The workshops showed us that co-design is particularly valuable when the behaviour we are trying to measure does not fit neatly into the assumptions built into existing questions. Rather than designing shisha questions around assumptions that come from cigarette smoking, we can start with how people actually experienced and remember their shisha use, and design from there.
