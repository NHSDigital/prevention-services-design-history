As part of our work on a digital service for lung cancer screening, we need to collect accurate information about people's smoking history. Earlier research showed that shisha does not fit neatly into questions designed around cigarette smoking, because people's patterns of use can be social, occasional and change significantly over time.

We had already learned a lot by testing our questions with people who smoke shisha. But testing our existing designs meant we were still starting with our own assumptions about what we needed to ask. We decided to try co-design instead: starting with people's lived experiences and asking them to help us explore how a shisha smoking history could be described and recorded.

In this post, we explain why we chose co-design, how we ran the workshops, and what we learned about using co-design as a research and design method.

## Why we chose co-design

We wanted to understand how people would approach the problem if we started with their experiences rather than our existing designs. Co-design gave us a way to explore this without immediately presenting participants with our assumptions about what the questions should look like.

Our main principle was:

> Start with someone's story, then work backwards to understand what we need to ask.

This helped us avoid showing our proposed solution too early and influencing how participants thought about the problem.

## What we did

We ran two workshops through community organisations in Hammersmith and Croydon.

The main part of each workshop had two activities.

### First, participants told their shisha story

Participants created a timeline showing how their shisha use had changed during their life.

They could include:

- when they started
- periods when they smoked more or less
- stopping and starting again
- social occasions
- important life events

![Hand-drawn timeline stretching from age 0 to the present. The participant started because they liked the smell of shisha, then began smoking with their father at age 18. They stopped because of coughing, started smoking a lot again while living in Iran, then stopped again after being diagnosed with asthma at around 28. After moving to the UK and their asthma improving, they started again at around 33. Since then, they have smoked shisha monthly or weekly with their partner.](timeline-task-hammersmith.png "An example from our Hammersmith workshop of one participant’s shisha history timeline")

The aim was to see how people naturally remembered and described their smoking history, so we told participants not to worry about being accurate.

### Then, participants designed their own questions

We asked participants to imagine that someone needed to record how much shisha they had smoked during their life.

We gave them blank NHS-style pages and asked:

> What would you ask someone so they could tell their shisha story accurately?

[insert image here]

This gave participants something practical to create, rather than asking them what they thought of our ideas.

The conversations that happened while people completed the activities were just as useful as what they put on paper. We took notes to record the important things people told us during these conversations.

### We used small groups

We split participants into groups of 2 or 3, with a member of the research team or a community leader supporting each table.

Supporting team members had prompts rather than a fixed script. This meant they knew what we wanted to learn but could follow interesting conversations as they happened.

Small groups also gave people more opportunities to contribute than relying on whole-room discussion.

### We worked with community organisations

Community leaders helped us:

- recruit participants
- find familiar community venues
- understand cultural considerations
- introduce the workshops
- support conversations
- translate where needed

This helped us run the workshops in places people already knew, with support from people they trusted.

We also planned for language differences from the beginning. We used both written and spoken instructions, provided translations of written instructions, allowed extra time for translation and gave participants the option to write in their preferred language.

### We changed the second workshop based on what we learned

We held a team debrief immediately after our first workshop.

One thing we changed was our opening question.

In the first workshop, we asked:

> “If somebody wanted to understand your relationship with shisha, what would they need 	to know?”

This worked, but people's answers overlapped with the timeline activity that followed.

For the second workshop, we made the opening broader:

> “What are the first three things you think of when you hear the word shisha?”

This gave us more insight into people's immediate associations with shisha, while leaving their personal history for the timeline activity.

Making this small change reduced repetition and gave each activity a clearer purpose.

## What we learned about using co-design

The workshops helped us understand more about shisha smoking history, but they also taught us about how to use co-design effectively. There are several things we would take into future co-design work:

- Start with lived experience before showing existing designs
- Give people something practical to create
- Use small groups to give everyone space to contribute
- Treat the activity as a conversation. What people say while creating something can be more useful than the finished output
- Give supporting team members prompts, not scripts, so they can follow useful conversations
- Plan for different language needs from the start
- Do not expect participants to produce the finished solution. Our job is to understand why people suggest particular approaches and use that evidence to make design decisions
- Debrief and make changes between workshops, rather than treating the workshop plan as fixed
- Tell people what happens next. Participants should be able to see how their contribution influenced the work

The next step was to bring together what people told us across both workshops and use it to improve how we ask about shisha smoking history in the digital service.
