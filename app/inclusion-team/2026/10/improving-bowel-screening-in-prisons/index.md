Lou Downe famously said that 10% of service design is designing, 90% is creating conditions for the service design to happen. It’s very much true in complex and complicated organisations, such as the NHS. The projects are usually full of interdependencies, and solutions can rarely be offered in isolation. It was no different in the recent project exploring improvements for bowel screening in prisons. The work was rooted in the principle that everyone has a right to an equitable healthcare service and supported key goals of [Fit for the future: 10 Year Health Plan for England](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/10-year-health-plan-for-england-fit-for-the-future/). 
This is how we navigated the complexity to reach impactful outcomes. 

### Service context

There are 122 prisons in England and Wales, with a prison population of about 86,000 people. They have 4 categories: from A (high security estates), through B, C (closed prisons) and D (open prisons). The prison population is mostly male (96%), with only 4% of it being female. They might be moving through the prison system often, which makes them quite a transient group. 

Primary healthcare in England for people in prisons is commissioned by NHS England.  It is provided locally within prisons. Everyone has access to it, but it's not always reflected on [the NHS Spine](https://digital.nhs.uk/services/spine/). Each of the prisons operates as "GP" and uses the Health and Justice Information System (HJIS), currently provided by SystmOne. Detained estates public health delivery is under [Specification 29 Section 7A](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/specification-29-section-7a-public-health-services-for-children-and-adults-in-secure-and-detained-settings-in-england-2023-2024/) by the national Health and Justice team. People who are in prisons have the right to equivalent healthcare. 

In bowel screening, the faecal occult blood test (FOBt) pathway follows [the requirements](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/bowel-cancer-screening-care-pathway/bowel-cancer-screening-care-pathway/) set up by the bowel screening programme. It is delivered by 5 bowel screening hubs and 64 bowel screening centres located across England.  

### The problem we were solving 
We aimed to investigate the impact of GP Spine registration – also known as General Medical Service (GMS) registration – on bowel screening in prisons. 

**General population bowel screening**

Bowel screening for the general public is automatic. Everyone who is eligible can participate, as long as they are: 
* between 50 and 74 years old 
* registered with a GP on Spine. 

For the prison population, bowel screening depends on patient choice around GP registration, or it might depend on manual processes in prisons.  

**Prison population bowel screening**

Currently, people who are being detained in prison can decide whether to be GMS registered or not. There are three possible outcomes of this decision: 
* When someone says “yes” to GMS registration and record sharing, their prison GP is noted on Spine and healthcare information becomes known to the prison healthcare team
* When someone says “no” to GMS registration, they still have access to the prison GP, and either: 
  - stay registered with their community GP on Spine if they were already registered with them
  - have no GP recorded on Spine (with GP status “unknown” or lack of information).
 
This introduces some complexity into how bowel screening for the prison population is managed and requires a manual workaround by the prison healthcare teams and bowel screening hubs to ensure those who are eligible receive an invitation.   

### Service approach for meaningful outcomes 

To deepen our understanding of the problem and find actionable opportunities, we followed user-centred design and an end-to-end service approach.  

![Dependencies of the project shown on a diagram: screening teams: Bowel screening programme, Bowel screening digital team, NHSE teams: Health and Justice Information Service, Health and Justice, Spine including Personal Demographic Service, Patient Index, Detained estates staff and healthcare teams: Prison staff (including admin and healthcare), Immigration Removal Centres staff, Secure Mental Health Institutions staff, Regional NHS teams: local commissioners, health promotion officers, bowel screening hubs, GPs](project-dependencies1.png)

We wanted to understand all the steps and operational processes that enabled them, including the manual workaround, so that we could identify opportunities. The steps included: 
* an entry into the prison,
* being invited to bowel screening and offered [a FIT kit](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/bowel-cancer-screening/),
* conditions in which it the test can be done and potential barriers,
* transferring or leaving the prison. 

**Research in prisons**

To understand the lived experience of the participants and what it takes to provide bowel screening in prisons, we ran extensive research. We visited 4 prisons to speak to those who were detained and the staff. We had 7 deep dives with prison healthcare staff and 2 cross-prison focus groups. Also, we ran in-depth interviews with 2 people with prior involvement with the criminal justice system. To learn more, read Designing for people in prison: what we learned through user research. 

**Working across organisational boundaries** 

To untangle organisational and delivery context, we worked in collaboration with around 80 subject matter experts (SMEs) and stakeholders across NHS England. They included the:  
* National Digital Screening team
* Bowel Screening Programme (clinical) team
* Health and Justice (H&J) national and regional teams
* Health and Justice Information System (HJIS) team 

We met with key stakeholders regularly during working group sessions. They allowed us to update on recent progress, receive feedback and align on direction. Also, they helped us build good relationships, trust and invaluable collaboration with experts in their domains. 

Additionally, we had 5 deep dive sessions with the bowel screening hubs, and we spoke to regional screening staff. The insights we gained from these sessions helped us deepen our understanding of the problem and identify system opportunities. 

**System analysis**

We had dived into dependencies between GMS registration, Spine/ Personal Demographic Service, HJIS system and bowel screening delivery, working with technical colleagues to understand the technical enablers. We undertook systems analysis alongside a review of relevant literature, policy, and clinical guidelines to develop a comprehensive understanding of the service landscape. We considered the context of some of the other screening pathways, such as cervical and breast screening, to gain a fuller picture of screening provision in prisons. 

### Imagining the future together 

Towards the end of the project, we ran an in-person recommendation feasibility review workshop with key stakeholders from different teams involved in delivery of bowel screening for the prison population. We used a simplified version of a service map to communicate process and insights and played it back to SMEs. As we were going through each of the stages, we were answering questions and taking further feedback. It brought everyone on the same page and gave us a great reference for the next part. 

We ran ideation aimed at addressing issues, followed by a review of the ideas. It proved to be an interactive and engaging way to enable open discussion about advantages and disadvantages of each, and honest conversation about checks and balances. After a couple of hours, we had favourites for final recommendations. 

The session was followed by an online check-in where we went through another review of the strategic recommendations. Collectively, these activities generated insights that directly informed and shaped the final recommendations. 

**Proposals to tackle key systemic issues** 

When we were considering high-level design of the strategic solutions, we aimed for them to: address the key challenges, improve the end-to-end bowel screening process in prison settings, and be feasible. They were followed by tactical recommendations. 

### The outcome 

Thanks to working across organisational boundaries, our collaboration with SMEs and stakeholders was very fruitful and considered solving [“the whole problem”](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/standards-and-technology/service-standard-points/2-and-3-work-towards-solving-a-whole-problem-and-provide-a-joined-up-experience/). We proposed options that were evidence-based and addressed pain points we heard about in research, as well as aimed at streamlining operational processes. They can’t exist in isolation though and require associated policy and business changes.
