Although this research was conducted specifically about bowel screening, many of the findings are relevant beyond bowel screening. This design history captures broader learning about screening, healthcare provision and the experiences of people in prison that may be useful for other programmes and services. For project-specific findings and recommendations, see the project design history.

## Why this work matters

People in prisons are recognised as at risk of facing health inequalities (they are explicitly included in the Equality and Health Inequalities Impact Assessment) and experience lower health outcomes compared with the general population.

Legally, people in prison are entitled to an ‘at least equivalent’ standard of healthcare as in the community, including access to preventative care such as screening. They are also entitled to informed choice and autonomy concerning their healthcare.

But prisons operate as highly structured systems with their own processes, systems and constraints. NHS England commissions healthcare in prisons, but there are many factors outside of healthcare’s remit within the prison environment that influence how services are delivered. These factors can affect access to screening and other healthcare services, so strong partnership working is essential.

## Key findings

These are the main themes we found, which are applicable to all types of screening and wider preventative services being delivered in this setting.

### No two prisons are the same

Significant variation exists across the estate. Factors include:

* security category, which shapes daily regimes, levels of movement, staff availability, how easily people can be reached etc.
* whether the site (and healthcare) is managed by a third party with their own priorities, governance etc.
* different prisons hold different types of people, for example individuals convicted of sexual offences, older people, or people with shorter sentences
* the type and layout of the building

### Things that make screening provision hard

* Sentence lengths – short sentences make it harder to offer and complete screening within limited timeframes. This is particularly acute in remand prisons (holding people awaiting trial or sentencing), where average lengths of stay could be days.

  > The average stay here is 10 days, it’s not enough time to do anything and I really worry about the people that could be walking around with cancer and this is our chance to find it
  > -- Healthcare staff

* Transfers and release – people move frequently between prisons or leave custody, sometimes unexpectedly, which disrupts continuity and impacts address records.
* Communication – all post is intercepted and healthcare-related letters are sent to healthcare teams first, which can cause delays in communications. A person in prison may be difficult to contact due to security protocols, work assignments, moving location in prison or transferring/leaving.
* Staff capacity - there can be high staff turnover (healthcare and justice staff) and stretched resources can make it challenging to prioritise screening alongside a long list of other demands. This affects consistency of delivery, staff awareness and training, and the level of support available for individuals.
* Daily routines are unpredictable - incidents, security concerns or operational priorities can quickly disrupt planned activity, making it difficult to rely on fixed appointments or timelines.

### Attitudes to healthcare vary widely, particularly around preventative care

* Some people engage with healthcare more when in prison than they would in the community, as they have less competing priorities.

  > I know a lot of people give themselves a bit of an MOT in prison… got the time to do it all
  > -- Person in prison

* For many, prison is their first sustained interaction with healthcare services, creating a significant window of opportunity to reach people who would not normally engage with screening.
* Others, particularly those serving longer sentences, may actively reject preventative healthcare, not seeing value in extending life expectancy.

  > Sadly, I’ve had people on life sentences say why would I want to extend my life Miss, I’m here for the rest of it
  > -- Healthcare staff

### Common barriers to screening are amplified

* Stigma around weakness – not wanting to appear weak with ill health.
* Fear of what would happen if results are abnormal.
  - Fear that their families would be more worried about them.
  - A higher rate of sexual trauma means people may not want to take part in anything that involves intimate areas (either initial test or following tests).
* Leaving prison to attend community appointments can be awkward and embarrassing.

  > They double cuff you, walk you round like a dog and everyone there knows why... I’m a proud man, it’s humiliating.
  > -- Person in prison

* Mistrust of the institution.

  > There is a lot of anxiety in jail… it’s a pressure cooker, people have time to think
  > -- Person in prison

* Privacy – sharing cells (including toilets) impacts use of self testing.
* Access needs are widespread but not necessarily known or provided for. People may be less likely to disclose access needs or may avoid being seen to receive different provision, making inclusive design both essential and challenging. Key stats ([reference (PDF)](https://resolution.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/NHSR-Perspectives-on-delivering-health-in-the-prison-and-justice-system-Slide-deck.pdf/)):
  - nearly three in five men (59%) and more than four in five women in prison (82%) say they have mental health problems
  - 57% of women, 41% of men and 30% of children in prison report having a disability
  - nearly a third of arriving prisoners (31%) had a neurodivergent need

  > A lot of the time people won’t tell us if they have an access need, they don’t want people to know and they won’t want to be seen with an easy read
  > -- Prison healthcare staff

This is a very quick wrap up of our high-level findings and we have a lot more to share.

This work was important because prisons present significant challenges for designing and delivering screening services, but are a vital opportunity to reach underserved populations.

Meaningful research and direct engagement with people in prison and staff is essential to grasp the true complexity of the setting and build real empathy for those living and working within it.

As you reflect on your own digital service, consider how these findings might impact it and how well it meets the needs of people in prison. If you’re unsure, feel free to come and discuss it with us.
