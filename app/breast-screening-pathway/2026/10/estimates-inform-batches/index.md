Making sure no one is missed is a key goal for every breast screening office (BSO). In a [recent round of interviews](/breast-screening-select/2026/10/ntdd-batch-usability-findings/), we deepened our understanding of how BSOs create batches of participants who will be invited for screening. 

## Round plans, estimates and batches

BSOs [adjust and refine their plans](/breast-screening-pathway/2026/03/what-bsos-told-us-about-forecasting-and-planning/), going from approximate to specific:

- a round plan typically looks 3 years ahead 
- estimates look 1 year ahead and are based on annual population figures
- batches look 6 to 8 weeks ahead and set out who will be invited

The round plan is adjusted based on estimates and round length. Estimates are done once a year. Batching is a more frequent activity, with BSOs telling us they specify 1 to 3 batches a week. Some BSOs create a batch for each GP practice. Others group several GP practices into a batch. One BSO that visits each location every year told us they usually include about 4.

## Comparing numbers as a safety check

If the estimates match the batches within tens of people, BSOs know they are on track.

For smaller BSOs if the numbers are out by 100 or more, they know something is off. Staff interrogate the figures and check for errors or GP practice mergers. For larger BSOs, 100 could be an acceptable difference. 

Over time, experienced staff develop a sense of what a safe difference is, but this knowledge can be hard to pass on.

> [!NOTE]
> "The documentation for how I do it is not robust, I’m not sure someone could pick this up. I learned by osmosis and there are lots of 'unless this…' or 'if this…'"

## Working in pairs

One staff member prepares the yearly estimate once the annual population figures come through. These estimates are often done in spreadsheets. 

Another staff member takes those estimates to 'specify the batches' in NBSS, then 'create batches' in BS Select. 

The people working together are often a manager and their deputy: for example, a programme manager and deputy, an admin lead and deputy or an office manager and deputy.

Their goal is to check each other's work, refine estimates, move towards more precise numbers, spot outliers and make sure no one is missed.

> [!NOTE]
> “I will do the specification, then run that past the admin lead. If she is happy with what I’ve estimated against her round plan of population estimates, we know it’s right. If it’s way out, then we review”

## BSOs do it differently

Each BSO has its own process and way of doing things. In London, each of the 6 BSOs sends its requirements to the hub, which specifies the batches on their behalf. 

Some BSOs use ages to set who is invited, while others use dates of birth. Some split their population by month of birth and screen a third of their cohort once a year. 

> [!NOTE]
> "It's a 3-year screening round and we split it into 3 one-year batches by month of birth: those born from January to April, May to August and September to December"

## Small changes have big implications

The interviews showed that even small changes to batching screens on BS Select can affect policies, training and processes beyond what we had anticipated.

## What this means for Rubie

Rubie (Run breast screening in England) will need to support BSOs in going from population estimates to batches, helping staff gain certainty or detect where numbers are out and ideally, why.

Rubie teams will need to decide which local variations to support and where to steer towards a simpler standardised process, which will help BSOs become more resilient in the face of staff changes.
