Bringing agile methodologies to established ways of working is tough. The ceremonies, the jargon, and the sheer amount of Post-it notes used can be difficult to get your head around.  

NHS England has been researching, designing and building its own tech to replace the National Breast Screening System, rather than procuring from an outsourced company. This means several multidisciplinary agile teams have been working alongside the established NHS breast screening programme team and clinicians.  

We’ve been reflecting on how agile works in the NHS when healthcare is a necessarily risk-averse and documentation-heavy environment. Here are the questions we’ve worked through together. 

## Why aren't we gathering requirements and signing them off? 

If you've outsourced build projects before, your expectation may be that you provide – or oversee the gathering of – requirements. In agile working, teams work to understand the needs of users before identifying what the new system or service will do. This way of designing ‘from the bottom up’ is different to outsourced solutions which are often ‘top down’ - taking existing software and adding functionality. 

Something that happens when you build services inhouse is that you see much more of the ‘messy middle’. This looks chaotic compared to the cleaner version an outsourced company shows you during progress meetings.  

The benefit of this is that it gives opportunities to influence and shape the product much more. It opens up space to question existing rules or beliefs about the way things are always done and build something that’s more efficient, or easier to maintain.  

**The tension:** Seeing deliverables at the start of a project vs jointly agreeing them after research has taken place 
**The learning:** Working in the open feels exposing. The aim is to build support and course correct as quickly as possible, not to poke holes in existing ways of doing things. We needed to be more overt about the belief that everybody did the best they could using the knowledge they had at the time.

## Why do we work in the open? 

Building a library of work, learning, reflections – and even code – is primarily about getting a shared understanding of the work. It also helps by inviting extra information, considerations or critique so that teams can course correct where they need to.  

This helps to reduce risk – which is a goal regardless of which methodology a team uses – it's just a different way of doing it. Working in the open also contributes to gaining support for the work by helping people understand why decisions are being made. Advocacy starts with understanding.  

This library of work is also useful to future teams who may be the ones finally building the new solution (sometimes projects are paused and all the knowledge gained is lost when the team disbands: working in the open is like insurance against that). 

**The tension:** Wanting to share openly vs keeping things internal 
**The learning:** Working in the open feels exposing. The aim is to build support and course correct as quickly as possible, not to poke holes in existing ways of doing things. We needed to be more overt about the belief that everybody did the best they could using the knowledge they had at the time. 

## Why do we have to reprioritise work all the time?   

It takes time to get a full picture of the interconnectedness of things. Sometimes realisations or information surfaces at different times, which affects which work will need to be done first, to allow for other things to come later.  

Once we understand what's needed, we can look to see whether we can start reusing components, patterns, tech, solutions from elsewhere, which saves time, effort and costs. 

**The tension:** Rigidly following a plan with static requirements vs designing for a dynamic complex world in small increments 
**The learning:** The question of ‘when will this be done by’ is very difficult to let go of. Our desire to see a plan is deeply ingrained. We learned that when the roadmap was created, everyone felt more calm and the workflow became more predictable. 

## There are so many meetings and chats going on: I can't keep up 

It is stressful trying to keep on top of every chat, message, meeting and email. Our teams have been filling in the gaps in their understanding and knowledge by asking programme teams - though it hasn't always been clear what all the different meetings are for, and who should attend which one. 

**The tension:** Attending every meeting vs going to the ones that you want/need to contribute to 
**The learning:** Curating online and offline places for people to meet and talk has helped to build relationships and increase trust. Being clear what different types of meeting are for gives people the chance to prepare.

## We’re not done learning yet 

What we’ve learned so far about using agile practices in digital screening is that it feels unsettling, but the constant course correction it allows is incredibly useful. It stops us building things that aren’t feasible, and helps us recognise where we need to spend more time figuring things out. The NHS is complex, and agile allows for this complexity without derailing timescales or scope.  

We don’t get neatly-planned timescales and checklists that give a sense of security, but can get outdated quickly. We do get lots of interaction and collaboration, resulting in products that are being thought through from several angles and rigorously tested. This has also resulted in building trust and creating strong relationships.    

The main thing we’re still learning is that all models are wrong, but some of them are useful. By reviewing our practice and working styles, we’re trying to be less dogmatic about agile and keeping pragmatic about what works for us, collectively.
